What the research says about plastic waste in building materials

ECOBLOCK mixes shredded plastic into an interlocking cement block. This page explains the two published papers we used as reference, in plain language, and shows how each one connects to our work.

Team 5 · Department of Civil Engineering, St Joseph Engineering College, Mangaluru · Building Material & Construction (1BCIV304)

Use of recycled plastic water bottles in concrete blocks

Sina Safinia and Amani Alkalbani. Procedia Engineering 164 (2016): 214–221. doi:10.1016/j.proeng.2016.11.612

Type
Experimental study
Where
Oman
Presented at
Creative Construction Conference 2016
Material studied
Plastic water bottles in hollow concrete blocks

The problem

Concrete blocks are one of the most common building units in Oman. At the same time, bottled water is widely consumed there, so plastic bottles pile up and landfills overflow. The authors ask a simple question: can used water bottles go inside a concrete block and still give a block that is acceptable for building?

What they did

Instead of mixing plastic into the concrete, the authors placed whole plastic bottles inside the block. The bottles create empty spaces (voids) at equal distances, and concrete is placed around each bottle. The strength of these blocks was then tested and compared with the hollow concrete blocks sold in the local Omani market. Compressive strength is found by dividing the maximum load a specimen carries by its loaded area.

The experiments used three concrete batches, from which a few concrete cylinders were cast for compression testing.

What they found

  • Blocks made with plastic bottles reached acceptable strength for hollow concrete blocks.
  • Compared with the local Omani hollow concrete blocks, the bottle blocks showed a 57% difference in strength, in favour of the bottle blocks.
  • The bottles may also add thermal insulation, which matters in Oman’s hot desert climate because it can cut the electricity used for cooling.

In one line: putting plastic bottles into a concrete block can keep the block strong enough, reduce plastic waste, and help keep the building cooler.

How it connects to ECOBLOCK

This paper shows that plastic waste can go into masonry blocks without ruining their strength, which is the same belief behind our project. The difference is the method: the paper uses whole bottles as void formers, while ECOBLOCK mixes shredded plastic into the cement–M-sand mortar.

Exploration of waste plastic bottles use in construction

Donald Kwabena Dadzie, Abdul K. Kaliluthin, and D. Raj Kumar. Civil Engineering Journal 6.11 (2020): 2262–2272. doi:10.28991/cej-2020-03091616

Type
Review paper
Authors based in
Ghana and India
Published
November 2020
Focus
Waste plastic bottles as a construction material

The problem

Many developing countries, Ghana among them, have a housing shortage, while natural construction materials such as sand and stone are being used up. At the same time, plastic bottle waste keeps growing. The paper looks at whether this waste can help with both problems.

What they did

This is not a single experiment. It is a review: the authors gather and compare earlier work from different places on how waste plastic bottles have been used in building. One approach they describe is filling bottles with soil, sand or other solid waste and using them like bricks or blocks, bound together with mortar to form masonry walls.

What they concluded

  • Lower cost. Waste bottles are cheaper to obtain than most conventional materials, so concrete or brick containing bottles needs smaller quantities of conventional material and costs less.
  • Environmental benefit and energy saving. Walls built with plastic bottles help keep room temperature steady, which saves energy and works like a simple thermal control system.
  • Less pressure on natural resources. Reusing bottles reduces how much new material has to be extracted.

In one line: waste plastic bottles are a cheap, widely available material that can lower building costs, save energy and ease the demand for natural resources.

How it connects to ECOBLOCK

This review supports the “why” of our project: cost, waste diversion and thermal comfort. Our problem statement lists exactly these concerns for small residential and community buildings, and our comparison table notes lower thermal conductivity as an expected benefit of plastic in the block.

Where ECOBLOCK fits

Both papers use plastic bottles as large pieces or fillers. ECOBLOCK takes a different route, using shredded plastic mixed into the mortar of an interlocking block, so the plastic acts more like small fibres than like voids.

  • Mix ratio 1 : 2 (cement : M-sand), 5 × 5 × 5 cm cube specimens.
  • Shredded plastic: 13 g per 3-block batch (4.33 g per block).
  • First batch, 7-day compressive strength: 16.36 MPa at a peak load of 40.9 kN.
  • Failure was ductile, with plastic bridging the cracks instead of the cube shattering suddenly.
  • Next: a higher plastic ratio and partial cement replacement with fly ash.

The papers above come from different test set-ups, so their numbers should not be compared directly with ours. They are used as background that supports the idea, not as a benchmark.
